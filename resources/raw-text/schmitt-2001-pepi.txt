Abstract
A method is described which enables the calculation of statistical properties of geodynamo models. The result is a
Fokker–Planck equation for the probability distribution of the dipole moment. The method only requires a non-oscillatory,
predominantly dipolar magnetic ﬁeld and helicity ﬂuctuations. The ﬂuctuations perturb the fundamental dynamo mode and
lead to the excitation of higher modes. This results in stochastic oscillations of the dipole ﬁeld amplitude in a bistable poten-
tial with minima representing normal and reversed polarity, and occasional jumps between them. The shape of the potential
is determined by supercritical dynamo excitation and nonlinear limitation of ﬁeld growth. Application of the method to a
mean-ﬁeld dynamo model with random ﬂuctuations of the helicity parameter α reproduces the observed relation between the
secular variation and the reversal rate of the geomagnetic ﬁeld, as well as the amplitude distribution of the dipole ﬁeld inferred
from the Sint-800 record. © 2001 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights reserved.
Keywords: Dynamo model; Helicity ﬂuctuations; Reversals; Secular variation
1. Introduction
One of the most fascinating topics of geomagnetism
is that the Earth has reversed the polarity of its almost
dipolar magnetic ﬁeld many times in the past at irreg-
ular intervals of 105 to 107 years (Jacobs, 1994; Mer-
rill et al., 1996). Reversals are fast events lasting only
a few thousand years (Bogue and Merrill, 1992). In
between reversals the ﬁeld strength ﬂuctuates consid-
erably (Valet and Meynadier, 1993; Guyodo and Valet,
1999). There are also episodes of small dipole moment

Our objective is to study the long-term statistical

core, the presence of helicity and large ﬂuctuations.
This leads to a dynamo model which allows us to
simulate many reversals over long periods of typically
108 years.

allowing the α-coefﬁcient to be a decreasing function

The theory of the Fokker–Planck equation is well
known (Van Kampen, 1992). Eq. (3) is identical to the
Fokker–Planck equation of a heavily damped particle
in a bistable potential U where −∂U/∂a = S (Fig. 2).
The potential is symmetric with respect to a = 0, and
this is ultimately due to the ±B invariance of the MHD
equations. At a = 0 there is a central hill due to the
supercriticality, and the side walls stem from the non-
linearity. Most of the time the dipole mode resides in
either of the wells, representing normal and reversed
polarity, respectively. The ﬂuctuations, δα, make the
fundamental mode amplitude perform a random mo-
tion around one of the stable minima of the potential,
with occasional jumps over the hill. The geodynamo
is thus pictured as a bistable oscillator. It can be
shown that reversals are always fast events, caused by
the interaction of the dipole mode with the overtones.

Recently, the so-called Sint-800 record of the vir-
tual axial dipole moment (VADM) in the past 800,000
years has become available (Guyodo and Valet, 1999).
The VADM represents the surface values of the l = 1,
m = 0 spherical harmonic of the poloidal ﬁeld. We
argue, that within the error bounds, the VADM is
proportional to the dipole amplitude a0 in our model.
Because of axisymmetry, m = 0. We note that a0
gives the time dependence of the toroidal and poloidal
ﬁeld of the fundamental mode. The dominating con-
tribution to a0 is l = 1, while l = 3 contributes about
20% to the amplitude, and higher l’s are negligible.
The mean amplitude a2 of the next antisymmetric
dynamo mode, which is only temporarily excited by
the ﬂuctuations and otherwise decaying, is approx-
imately 10% of a0. Both the l = 3 part of a0 as
well as a2 fall off with height faster than the l = 1
contribution of a0. We thus estimate that the error in
identifying a0 with the l = 1, m = 0 harmonic at the
surface is less than 10%. Since this is about the ob-
servational error of the VADM record, we conclude
that, within the error bounds, a0, and the VADM are
proportional.
The VADM amplitude exhibits a relative variance
of 0.07. From the proportionality of the VADM and
the dipole amplitude a, we infer that this corresponds
to a forcing parameter f = 5.4 in our model, which
yields a reversal time of 500 diffusion times or ∼1
million years, and is consistent with the length of the
Brunhes chron which now lasts for 780,000 years. By
binning the Sint-800 data we have derived the dipole
amplitude distribution and we compared the result
with the theoretical distribution (4) (see Fig. 4). The
close matching of the two demonstrates that our the-
oretical analysis correctly interprets the geomagnetic
data, and supports, by implication, the underlying
physics of the model.

6. Discussion
In our model linear multiplicative noise causes
the dipole amplitude to perform a random walk of
restricted amplitude around the nonlinear equilib-
rium. During a reversal the model evolves as a linear
system since a0 ≃0 in Eq. (2). But since the ﬂuctua-
tions are a manifestation of nonlinear turbulence, the
dipole amplitude variations and the reversals are ul-
timately caused by nonlinear effects. The essence of
our approach is the idea that on the fast time scale of
the eddy-turnover time the effect of these nonlineari-
ties is virtually indistinguishable from that of a noise
term, so that the modelling may proceed in this way,
without much loss of information. For longer time
scales this is no longer true and nonlinearities remain
essential. This is in fact a well known and powerful
approach in many areas of physics. It follows that a
unique physical cause for a reversal does not exist in
our model, and given its success the implication is
that that may hold for the geodynamo as well.
Another advantage of our approach is that some
of the properties that are inaccessible to theoretical
analysis in fully nonlinear models may now actually
be computed. But of course not all properties may
be had in this way. For example, it is not possible
to predict the magnitude of the geomagnetic ﬁeld of
∼0.5 G without a fully nonlinear model.
Although our dynamo model is clearly highly
simpliﬁed, the statistical analysis can equally well be
applied to more realistic models, such as 2D models.
This is because the starting point, Eq. (2), features
only the eigenvalues and certain overlap integrals of
the eigenfunctions. The only requirement for its ap-
plicability is the existence of a source of ﬂuctuations,
and a dominant stationary dipole mode. In this respect
the analysis presented here serves as a prototype.
